An open engineering workbench · Started October 2026

The model ran.
But is it right?

A solver finishing its job is not the same as an engineering result making sense. I started Open Energy Methods to explore that gap: build small tools, test the assumptions, and publish what I find.

I work around thermodynamics, energy modelling and numerical methods. This is a place to ask better questions of the numbers—not to sell a seal of approval.

On the bench / 01

EnerLint, including the mistake it cannot catch.

EnerLint is my first small experiment: a Python tool that checks declared mass and energy balances from exported model results. It is independent of the solver's own convergence flag—but it still depends on the data and assumptions you give it.

01 / CLOSED

A simple turbine balances.

For a hand-constructed turbine example, the mass and first-law residuals are both zero. The declared checks pass.

PASS_IN_SCOPE0 kg/s · 0 W
02 / BROKEN

Flip the compressor work sign.

The selected mutation produces a −480,000 W energy residual. The checker notices this specific inconsistency.

03 / THE INTERESTING ONE

Change the flows and work together.

Scale both compressor mass flows and its shaft work by 10%. The model's operating point changes, yet both balance equations still close. EnerLint passes it.

A note from the person making this

I like the part of engineering where you have to ask why.

I'm Abhijith, an energy engineer interested in thermodynamics, computational models, and what makes a numerical result defensible.

Working on energy-system and thermodynamic modelling projects led me to keep returning to a basic question: what have we actually checked when a model reports success?

I don't have a grand answer. I have a first tool, a set of small tests, and a list of uncomfortable edge cases. I'd rather show those than pretend the work is further along than it is.

Open Energy Methods is an umbrella for that kind of work. EnerLint is the starting point, not the whole destination.
